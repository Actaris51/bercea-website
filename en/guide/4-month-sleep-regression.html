The 4-month sleep regression:
myth or reality?

📖 5 min read 🔬 Based on 2024 research Updated: July 2026

Your baby is approaching 4 months and you're dreading the famous "sleep regression" everyone talks about? Good news: recent science is far more reassuring than parenting forums. Here's what actually happens.

What research discovered in 2024

Dr. Jodi Mindell (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia), one of the most cited researchers in pediatric sleep, analyzed data from thousands of mothers. Her finding upends the received wisdom: there is no universal spike in night wakings at 4 months — nor at 8, 12 or 18 months.

In practice, only 28 to 30% of parents report a deterioration in sleep between 3 and 5 months. In other words: roughly 7 in 10 babies go through this period without any notable disruption. If everyone is predicting catastrophic nights for you, know that it is neither universal nor inevitable.

💡 Key takeaway: the word "regression" is misleading. It isn't a step backwards but a maturation — your baby's sleep is becoming more sophisticated, not less.

What actually changes around 3-4 months

While the "regression" isn't universal, the biological transformation is real and concerns every baby:

  • Sleep architecture matures: baby moves from immature cycles (active / quiet sleep) to adult-like cycles (light, deep and REM sleep)
  • Melatonin kicks in: the circadian rhythm starts regulating sleep, guided by light and regular schedules
  • Brief arousals appear: between two cycles, baby surfaces into very light sleep — this is normal and will last their whole life

The sensitive point is those brief arousals: a baby who can resettle alone sails through them without you noticing. A baby who needs help falling asleep (rocking, feeding) will ask for that same help at every cycle transition — sometimes every 45 minutes.

How to support the transition

1. The bedtime routine — the best-validated tool

It's the intervention with the strongest scientific evidence. A 2024 clinical study of 306 infants showed a significant gain in night sleep for babies with a structured routine: bath, massage, story, cuddle, dim lights. The brain learns to anticipate sleep.

2. Respect the wake windows

Between 3 and 4 months, the wake window sits between 1.5 and 2.5 hours; between 4 and 6 months, between 2 and 3 hours. A baby put down too late (high cortisol) wakes more at night. See the complete wake windows chart.

3. Put baby down calm, but awake

Gradually help your baby fall asleep in their bed rather than in your arms. They'll learn to resettle alone during brief arousals between cycles — the key skill for full nights.

4. Observe without over-interpreting

A few bad nights don't mean "the regression" is here to stay for months. In affected babies, disruptions typically last 2 to 6 weeks — the time it takes to adapt.

When to seek advice

If the wakings come with unusual crying, fever, feeding difficulties or a change in daytime behavior, talk to your pediatrician: the cause may lie elsewhere (reflux, ear infection, teething...).
